The simulator

Sample: MgO(001)

A fingernail-sized crystal of magnesium oxide (big dots oxygen, small dots magnesium): a square grid that repeats every 4.212 Å. It doesn’t conduct, so resistance measurements see only the metal.

Molybdenum’s grid fits on it turned 45°, only 5.7% off, so a hot film can copy the crystal underneath: epitaxy. See the (001) face

  1. Clean

    Ultrasonic baths of trichloroethylene, acetone, isopropyl alcohol, then DI water, 15 min each.

  2. Mount

    Blow dry with nitrogen; stick it to a Mo holder with silver paint.

  3. Load

    In through the load lock; the main chamber stays at 10⁻⁹ Torr.

  4. Bake

    1,000 °C for 1 hour to drive off water and gas on the surface.

  5. Coat

    Mo in 20 mTorr argon, sample at 900 °C and spinning. This is the step the simulator runs.

  6. Anneal

    1,000 °C for 2 hours so the crystal smooths out its flaws.

  7. Cool

    Back to room temperature slowly, over about 12 hours.

  8. Measure

    Four-point probe without breaking vacuum, X-ray diffraction for alignment, X-ray reflectivity for thickness.

Steps follow a published procedure from the Gall group at RPI: epitaxial Mo(001) and Mo(011) layers. Films in the study ran from about 4 to 400 nm thick.

Why heat the MgO to 900 °C?

Same Mo run, hot vs. room temperature. Watch “Lines up with the sample’s crystal?” and the gaps.

Does the sample need to be a crystal?

Hot Mo on MgO, then on glass, which has no pattern for the film to copy.

Why use so little gas?

Copper with a little argon, then a lot. Watch “Atoms that fly straight” and the gaps.

Why put titanium down first?

Copper alone, then on a thin Ti layer, which helps it stick (the chemistry isn’t simulated).

Sputtering

The machine is a sealed chamber pumped down to almost no air, with a little argon gas let back in. Electricity turns the argon in front of a metal target into a glowing plasma. Argon ions slam into the target and knock metal atoms loose, and those atoms land on the sample and build up a coating. This machine has two targets, called guns. Each has a shutter, so it can lay one metal on top of another without opening the chamber.

Gas

Metal atoms have to fly through the argon to reach the sample. With less gas they fly straight and fast, and the coating comes out solid. With more gas they bump into argon over and over, slow down, and land from random directions, which leaves gaps.

Heat

After an atom lands, it can move a little before more atoms bury it. How far depends on how hot the sample is compared with the metal’s melting point. Room temperature is fairly warm for copper, which melts at 1,085 °C, but very cold for tungsten, which melts at 3,422 °C. So the same settings give a solid copper coating and a porous tungsten one.

Angle

If the sample is tilted far over, atoms arrive at a low angle and every bump shadows the spot behind it, so the coating grows as slanted columns with gaps between them. Spinning the sample while it grows straightens the columns into posts.

For the curious: the distance between gas hits comes from kinetic theory (λ = kT/√2πd²p, d ≈ 0.35 nm). An atom counts as flying straight if it hits argon fewer than 4 times. Guns sit 20° off the sample’s axis. Sputter yields at 600 eV Ar⁺ are from Angstrom Sciences. Coating types follow Thornton’s structure zone model (1974) with approximate boundaries. Coating speeds are rough estimates for a small lab gun, and the close-up is a 2D slice, so treat the numbers as trends.
