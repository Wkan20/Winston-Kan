What the model does

Every atom follows three rules. It flies in a straight line at the deposition angle. It sticks at the first spot where it touches the substrate or another atom. If the substrate is hot, it can hop a few times toward spots where it has more neighbors, since more bonds means lower energy.

That's all. The columns aren't programmed in. They come from shadowing: at a steep angle, any bump catches atoms that would have landed behind it, so bumps grow into columns and the space behind them stays empty.

Tait's rule is a geometric estimate of how far GLAD columns lean. This model's columns usually lean a little less, which is typical of simple grid models. Real films also depend on things the model leaves out, like the material, ion energy, and the full 3D geometry.

Experiments to try

  1. Start at 0° and step up to 85°. Watch density fall as shadowing takes over.
  2. At 85°, raise the temperature. Hopping atoms fill some gaps, but shadowing still wins at steep angles.
  3. Turn on continuous rotation. The flux now comes from every side, so columns grow straight up as posts.
  4. Try flip back and forth. Each flip changes which way the columns lean, building zigzags.
  5. At 0°, compare cold and hot. This is why the substrate heater matters even without GLAD.
